CELL·NET

A massively parallel cellular automaton engine. Every cell is its own circuit; this console is its operator panel, from software twin to live silicon.

Each dot runs one rule, reading only its eight neighbors. On a CPU that's 1,600 sequential updates per frame. On the FPGA it's one clock edge: every dot is its own coil-driven cell. This board is the software twin of that fabric: what you draw here becomes the pattern the real flip-dots throw.

The Drossel-Schwabl forest fire model: the console's first rule that is multi-state and probabilistic. Each cell is empty, a tree, or on fire. Fire burns out in one step, spreads to any tree touching it, and strikes as rare lightning. In hardware that's a 2-bit register per cell, an 8-input OR for "any neighbor burning" (cheaper than Life's popcount), and per-cell taps off an LFSR for the randomness. Click the board to drop lightning yourself.

The ratio p/f sets the character of the system: rare lightning over fast growth means the forest fills in and burns in huge, connected sweeps; frequent lightning keeps fires small before regions can connect. This is the same percolation trade-off behind real fire management, and it needs no global coordinator: every cell still reads only its eight neighbors, which is why it drops onto the same FPGA fabric as Life.

As of Phase 4.5 the chip listens as well as talks: one 0x55 command byte plus a full grid snapshot, and seed_loader.v drops your pattern into the fabric while the stream keeps running. What comes back on this board a moment later is that pattern evolving in real silicon.

The engine outputs a grid of bits every clock. The interesting question is what physical medium throws those bits. Here are the real options, from the one that best matches the "one coil per cell" thesis to the honest prototyping baseline, each with how the FPGA would actually drive it.

Flip-Dot Matrix

Primary target

One bistable electromagnetic disc per pixel: black on one face, phosphor-yellow on the other. A pulse latches it; it then holds with zero power. FPGA drives panels through serial coil drivers (AlfaZeta / ULN2803 + shift registers): one clock streams a whole frame, so the CA's parallel update maps straight onto the panel. The mechanical clack is the point.

A

Split-Flap (Solari)

Text · scores

Rotating character flaps, one small motor/coil per module. Too coarse for a dense pixel grid, but perfect for throwing the engine's readouts (generation count, population) as a departure-board display. FPGA times the flap stepping sequence.

01

Vacuum Fluorescent

Glow · persistence

Phosphor anodes under a heated filament: that cyan-green glow with soft persistence. Needs roughly 20 to 30 V of anode/grid drive, so FPGA plus a boost converter and a high-voltage driver. Beautiful for a compact status panel beside the main board.

07

Nixie Tubes

Counters

Cold-cathode neon, one shaped cathode per digit, roughly 170 V DC. Not a matrix, so not the grid, but the right medium for the counters: live generation and peak-dwell in orange neon. FPGA + HV supply + a driver like the K155ID1.

LED Dot Matrix

Baseline

Cheapest, fastest, no moving parts. HUB75 panels refresh in microseconds and FPGAs drive them brilliantly. This is what you prototype the fabric on first: prove the grid, then graduate the output to flip-dots.

Plan: prove the parallel fabric on a cheap LED/HUB75 panel first (it just wants bits, fast), then swap the output stage to a flip-dot panel once the Verilog grid is solid. The cell logic doesn't change, only the driver on the last stage does. That separation (compute fabric vs. output medium) is exactly how you'd argue the design in a writeup.
